Rafflee: building a competition experience from scratch

There was no legacy interface to tidy up and no established pattern to copy. We had to define what Rafflee should feel like, how people would discover a competition, and how the product could make entering feel simple, exciting, and trustworthy.

01 · The starting point

Starting with a blank canvas

Rafflee was a true 0→1 challenge. Instead of inheriting an existing information architecture or interaction model, we had to decide what the product was, what mattered on day one, and what should stay out of the way.

The design problem was bigger than making a raffle interface look attractive. A user needs to understand the prize, the cost of entry, the state of the competition, and what happens next — quickly enough to feel confident before committing.

01

Make the prize obvious

The prize is the reason to stop scrolling. It gets visual priority and a clear, consistent presentation.

02

Make entry understandable

Price, remaining time, progress and the primary action should be scannable without interpretation.

03

Earn trust at every step

Draw information, competition status and outcome visibility belong in the product experience, not buried at the end.

02 · The experience model

Designing the path from curiosity to entry

The core journey became a simple loop: discover → evaluate → enter → track → outcome. Every screen has a job in moving the user through that loop.

03 · Information architecture

Separating browsing from commitment

One of the important structural decisions was to keep discovery lightweight and move detailed information into the competition page. That lets the listing page work like a catalogue while the detail page answers the questions that actually affect entry.

Discovery

Scan first

04 · The interface

A system that could scale beyond one competition

Rather than designing a collection of one-off screens, I treated the experience as a reusable system: competition cards, status patterns, entry controls, progress indicators, result states and trust surfaces all share the same visual grammar.

05 · Trust by design

Excitement gets attention. Clarity earns the entry.

Competition products have a natural tension: the experience should feel exciting, but the mechanics cannot feel ambiguous. I designed trust into the hierarchy rather than treating it as a legal afterthought.

↗

Visible status

Users can understand whether a competition is live, closing, or complete without hunting for the information.

✓

Clear commitment

The entry step makes the cost and selected quantity explicit before confirmation.

◎

Outcome continuity

The experience doesn't end at payment; tracking and winner/result states complete the product loop.

06 · What I learned

0→1 design is mostly deciding what not to build

Rafflee reinforced a lesson I carry into every new product: when there is no legacy to constrain you, the temptation is to design everything. The better approach is to define the smallest coherent system that makes the core promise believable.

That meant keeping the primary journey focused, creating reusable patterns early, and letting visual excitement support — rather than compete with — the information users need to make a decision.
